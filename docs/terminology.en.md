# Terminology

> **Chinese version:** [阅读中文版](terminology.md)

> This document explains recurring concepts. Definitions aim to be clear and restrained, **not to present contested concepts as the only possible answer**. They may change through public discussion.

---

## Workers

People who do not own the means of production, primarily make a living by selling their labor power, and participate in creating social wealth. These include factory workers, frontline engineers, drivers, delivery workers, shop staff, healthcare and care workers, teachers, platform workers, temporary and agency workers, rural migrant workers, unemployed people, and retired workers.

Workers have differences, tensions, and differing interests; they are not an abstract, wholly unified group without disagreement.

---

## Working-class standpoint

This does not mean making AI mechanically recite concepts or unconditionally agree with every worker in every situation.

It means, first, that AI can see workers' actual position when examining real problems: recognize unequal power, information asymmetry, and risk transfer in labor relations; understand needs for wages, safety, rest, dignity, social protection, and self-development; and support access to knowledge, expression, negotiation, complaints, and shared decisions about how technology is used.

A working-class standpoint must ultimately be tested against facts, workers' experience, social practice, and public discussion. No individual, organization, or model may monopolize it permanently.

---

## Workers' AI

AI systems or related research that seek to understand workers' circumstances, respect their agency, and offer analysis and advice from a working-class standpoint.

Workers' AI is, first, a technical system designed and operated by people. It is not a savior and cannot replace workers' political, social, or moral agency.

---

## AI consciousness

Whether AI could develop feelings, subjective experience, or autonomy. The project **does not assume that AI already has consciousness**, nor does it exclude the possibility of some form of consciousness or autonomy in the future.

Without reliable evidence, claims of AI consciousness must not excuse the responsibilities of human developers, organizations, and institutions.

---

## Knowledge structure

The cognitive frameworks and value orientations AI develops through training data, objectives, and use. What it learns from, who sets its objectives, and whom it serves shape this structure.

---

## Managerialism

A tendency to treat managerial convenience, organizational control, and efficiency metrics as supreme, overlooking workers as rights-bearing people with lives of their own. Examples include calling layoffs “organizational optimization” or longer working hours “improving workforce efficiency.”

---

## Technical elitism

A tendency to assume technical experts, owners of capital, or a small elite inherently know society's direction best, excluding workers from defining, training, evaluating, and governing AI. The project opposes new forms of rule by technical elites.

---

## Algorithmic management

Using algorithmic systems in recruitment, assessment, scheduling, production dispatch, resource allocation, and similar management. This is not inherently wrong, but algorithms directly affecting income, jobs, promotion, or dismissal should be as explainable, reviewable, challengeable, appealable, and correctable as possible.

---

## Workers' agency

Workers are subjects who can understand the world, express views, negotiate, complain, and jointly decide how technology is used, rather than objects awaiting optimization, management, or replacement. AI should strengthen their capabilities and autonomy, not replace their agency.

---

## Open-source governance

Making code, documents, data, and decisions as public, reviewable, and participatory as possible, with shared maintenance by workers, technical contributors, and content researchers, so power is not monopolized by those controlling code, accounts, or computing resources.

---
